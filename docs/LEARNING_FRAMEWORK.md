# Learning Framework

## Principle

Wondervale models learning as a graph of abilities and concepts, not as four isolated school subjects.

A child-facing story may combine language, mathematics, science, and humanities without announcing subject boundaries.

## Learning cycle

Preferred progression:

**Encounter → Explore → Understand → Apply → Revisit → Transfer → Deepen**

A concept should often return after enough time has passed that the child must reconstruct or transfer it.

## Example capability families

### Language
- listening comprehension
- vocabulary in context
- reading fluency
- describing
- sequencing events
- inference
- storytelling
- conversation

### Number and mathematics
- quantity
- comparison
- number sense
- addition and subtraction
- multiplication foundations
- fractions
- estimation
- measurement
- spatial reasoning
- patterns
- data interpretation

### Scientific reasoning
- observation
- prediction
- classification
- cause and effect
- fair testing
- evidence
- systems
- model building
- explanation

### Humanities and social reasoning
- place and geography
- chronology
- culture
- needs and resources
- trade
- fairness
- perspective taking
- community
- change over time

## Skill roles in an episode

Each episode can declare:

- **primary** — core learning goal;
- **secondary** — meaningfully practiced;
- **revisit** — previously encountered skill returning in a new situation;
- **transfer** — prior skill required in a substantially different context.

## Age adaptation

A story should remain recognizable across ages 6–9 while challenge can vary.

Possible adaptations:

- reading length;
- number range;
- number of conditions;
- amount of scaffolding;
- vocabulary complexity;
- abstractness of explanation;
- independence of reasoning.

Avoid maintaining four completely separate story versions unless necessary.

## Assessment philosophy

Prefer evidence embedded in action:

- what the child predicts;
- what they try;
- whether they revise;
- how they explain;
- whether they can apply a concept elsewhere.

Do not make conventional quizzes the default proof of learning.

## Parent role

Parent prompts should elicit thinking rather than supply knowledge.

Examples:

- “What do you think will happen?”
- “What did you notice?”
- “Why do you think that?”
- “What changed?”
- “Can you think of something similar outside the game?”

## Content quality check

Every learning activity should answer:

1. Why does the child care in the story?
2. What thinking is required?
3. What feedback comes from the world?
4. What can the parent ask?
5. Where might this idea return later?
