# Learning Framework

## Principle

Wondervale models learning as a connected graph of concepts, skills, strategies, and dispositions rather than as four isolated school subjects.

A child-facing story may combine language, mathematics, science, and humanities without announcing subject boundaries.

## Four learning node types

### Concepts
Ideas the child comes to understand.

Examples:

- quantity;
- fractions;
- water flow;
- habitat;
- trade;
- chronology.

### Skills
Things the child can increasingly do.

Examples:

- measure;
- compare;
- read for meaning;
- sequence events;
- interpret a map.

### Reasoning strategies
Ways of approaching an unfamiliar problem.

Examples:

- observe before concluding;
- predict;
- test;
- compare evidence;
- revise an explanation;
- break a problem into parts.

### Dispositions
Patterns worth supporting through the experience, not scoring as personality traits.

Examples:

- curiosity;
- persistence;
- willingness to revise;
- perspective taking;
- asking for evidence;
- asking for help.

Do not turn dispositions into badges or behavior grades.

## Learning cycle

Preferred progression:

**Encounter → Explore → Understand → Apply → Revisit → Transfer → Deepen**

A revisit should change context, purpose, representation, or required reasoning rather than merely substitute different numbers or pictures.

## Skill roles in an episode

Each episode may declare:

- **primary** — one or two central learning goals;
- **secondary** — meaningfully practiced;
- **revisit** — previously encountered learning returning;
- **transfer** — prior learning required in a substantially different context.

Avoid using a long skill list to justify an overloaded episode.

## Reading/language is not age

A 7-year-old may have strong mathematical reasoning but need substantial reading support. Another child may read fluently but need more number scaffolding.

Do not collapse all challenge into one “age level.”

Future support can vary independently across:

- reading;
- spoken/listening language;
- number complexity;
- reasoning scaffolding;
- motor/accessibility needs.

The MVP can keep these simple while preserving the model.

## Learning evidence

Prefer evidence embedded in action and conversation:

- what the child predicts;
- what features they notice;
- what they try;
- whether they revise;
- how they explain;
- whether they use an earlier idea in a new context.

Not all evidence must be automatically scored.

The content design should still state what meaningful evidence would look like so activities do not accidentally measure only UI skill.

## Misconceptions

When useful, an episode can record plausible misconceptions or incomplete models.

These should inform:

- world feedback;
- hints;
- parent prompts;
- later revisit.

Do not treat every unexpected child answer as an error.

## Standards mapping

Wondervale may later map learning nodes to external curriculum standards for parent/educator reference.

Standards mapping should be metadata, not the narrative engine. The story should not be forced into a checklist merely to cover every standard.

## Parent role

Parent prompts should elicit thinking rather than supply knowledge.

Examples:

- “What do you think will happen?”
- “What did you notice?”
- “Why do you think that?”
- “What changed?”
- “Can you think of something similar outside the game?”

The best prompt often follows the child's attention rather than interrupting it.

## Content quality check

Every substantive learning activity should answer:

1. Why does the child care in the story?
2. What thinking is required?
3. What evidence of understanding could appear?
4. What feedback comes from the world?
5. What scaffold preserves thinking if the child is stuck?
6. What can the parent ask?
7. Where might this idea return later?
